# ML Paradigms for Joint Structure-Function Graph Modeling

*Literature review prepared for NeuroLens-RAG. Scope: the paired dataset of ~174 HCP Young Adult subjects with (1) structural connectomes from diffusion tractography on the Schaefer-300 parcellation and (2) functional ROI time series from ~15-minute naturalistic movie-watching fMRI on the same 300 nodes.*

## Executive Summary

The literature on modeling a fixed structural graph together with a dynamic functional signal splits cleanly along two axes that are easy to conflate: which **architecture** (the function-approximation block) is used, and which **learning paradigm/objective** (the probabilistic quantity being fit) is being solved. Most neuroimaging papers only report the architecture, but the more useful organizing question — the one Kevin Murphy's *Probabilistic Machine Learning* books push toward — is what quantity the loss function is actually estimating: p(y | x) for a label, p(future | past, graph) for forecasting, an implicit density ratio for contrastive objectives, or a full joint p(graph, signal) for generative models. Architecturally, the field has moved from spectral graph convolutions (Bruna et al. 2014; Defferrard, Bresson & Vandergheynst 2016, "ChebNet"; Kipf & Welling 2017, GCN) through general message-passing and attention (Veličković et al. 2018, GAT) to graph transformers that trade locality for global receptive field, motivated by well-documented message-passing limits (over-smoothing, over-squashing, bounded 1-WL expressiveness). A separate, more directly relevant lineage — spatiotemporal GNNs from traffic forecasting (Yu, Yin & Zhu 2018 STGCN; Li et al. 2018 DCRNN) and human action recognition (Yan, Xiong & Lin 2018 ST-GCN) — was built explicitly for "fixed graph + signal flowing over it in time," which is structurally identical to the structural-connectome-plus-movie-fMRI setting here. Neuroimaging-specific architectures (BrainNetCNN, BrainGNN, BrainGB, Brain Network Transformer, BolT, GraphS4mer) largely reuse these ideas but are almost all trained under a single paradigm — supervised discriminative classification/regression — leaving self-supervised, generative/predictive, and especially reinforcement-learning paradigms comparatively underexplored for connectome + naturalistic-fMRI data specifically. That gap is itself the most actionable finding: the project's paired structure-function dataset is well positioned for self-supervised or generative objectives (temporal contrastive predictive coding, masked graph/temporal modeling, or forecasting future ROI activity conditioned on the structural graph) that current brain-connectome papers apply only sparingly, mostly by import from computer vision/NLP/traffic-forecasting rather than as native contributions to this domain.

## 1. Historical Grounding: The Spectral/Geometric Deep Learning Lineage

The line of work the project owner's research background sits in begins with Bruna, Zaremba, Szlam & LeCun's **"Spectral Networks and Locally Connected Networks on Graphs"** (ICLR 2014, arXiv:1312.6203), which first generalized convolution to graphs via the eigenbasis of the graph Laplacian, at the cost of O(N) parameters per filter and no spatial localization guarantee. Defferrard, Bresson & Vandergheynst's **ChebNet** (NeurIPS 2016, arXiv:1606.09375, "Convolutional Neural Networks on Graphs with Fast Localized Spectral Filtering") fixed both problems by approximating spectral filters with truncated Chebyshev polynomials of the Laplacian, giving filters that are exactly K-hop localized and that cost O(E) rather than O(N²) — this is the paper that explicitly cites brain connectomes as a target irregular domain. Kipf & Welling's **GCN** (ICLR 2017, "Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks") then took the first-order (K=1) truncation of ChebNet and showed that this drastically simplified layer-wise propagation rule was sufficient for strong performance on citation-network node classification, and it became the default GNN building block for the following several years, including in early brain-connectome GNN papers.

The field's synthesis into a unifying theory came with Bronstein, Bruna, Cohen & Veličković's **"Geometric Deep Learning: Grids, Groups, Graphs, Geodesics, and Gauges"** (arXiv:2104.13478, 2021 proto-book, following their earlier 2017 IEEE Signal Processing Magazine survey "Geometric Deep Learning: Going beyond Euclidean data" by Bronstein, Bruna, LeCun, Szlam & Vandergheynst). The proto-book reframes CNNs, GNNs, RNNs, and Transformers as instances of a single blueprint: pick a domain, its symmetry group, and build a network equivariant to that group. This is the "blocks are interchangeable" observation the report keeps returning to — a GNN and a Transformer are two different equivariance choices (permutation-with-fixed-graph vs. permutation-over-a-set) applied to the same underlying learning problem, exactly analogous to how PML treats CNNs/RNNs/Transformers as interchangeable parameterizations of p(y|x).

Since 2018 the field moved past pure spectral convolution toward **message-passing neural networks** (the unifying framework of Gilmer et al. 2017) and **attention-based** variants: Veličković, Cucurull, Casanova, Romero, Liò & Bengio's **Graph Attention Network** (ICLR 2018, arXiv:1710.10903) replaced fixed Laplacian-derived weights with learned attention coefficients over neighborhoods, removing the need to know the full graph spectrum and enabling inductive generalization to unseen graphs. More recently, **graph transformers** have emerged as a response to well-documented weaknesses of message passing: bounded expressiveness (no more powerful than the 1-Weisfeiler-Lehman test), over-smoothing (node representations converge with depth), over-squashing (long-range information is compressed through narrow graph bottlenecks), and poor long-range dependency modeling (surveyed in, e.g., "Graph Transformers: A Survey," arXiv:2407.09777, and "A Comprehensive Survey on Deep Graph Representation Learning," arXiv:2304.05055). Graph transformers give every node a global receptive field via self-attention, at the cost of losing the graph's locality as a built-in inductive bias unless it is reintroduced via positional/structural encodings. For a 300-node structural connectome, this matters directly: message passing over the tractography graph enforces "information only flows along white-matter pathways," while a graph transformer can in principle attend between two ROIs with no direct structural edge — a real architectural choice, not just an implementation detail.

## 2. Architectures for Combining a Static Graph with Dynamic Node Signals

This is the section most directly applicable to structural-connectome-plus-movie-fMRI, because it is precisely the "fixed topology, time-varying signal on top of it" problem, first solved outside neuroscience.

**Traffic forecasting** produced the cleanest templates because road networks are static graphs with sensor readings that evolve over time — structurally identical to (structural connectome, ROI time series). Li, Yu, Shahabi & Liu's **DCRNN** (ICLR 2018, arXiv:1707.01926, "Diffusion Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network") models the spatial signal as a diffusion process (bidirectional random walks) over the graph and wraps this diffusion convolution inside a recurrent encoder-decoder (GRU-based) for the temporal dimension — i.e., GNN-as-spatial-operator nested inside an RNN. Yu, Yin & Zhu's **STGCN** (IJCAI 2018, "Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks") instead replaced the recurrent component with purely convolutional (1-D temporal + graph) gated blocks, showing that recurrence was not necessary for spatiotemporal graph forecasting and was often slower to train — an early instance of the now-common finding that attention/convolution can substitute for recurrence in sequence modeling.

**Human action recognition** produced the other major template: Yan, Xiong & Lin's **ST-GCN** (AAAI 2018, "Spatial Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks for Skeleton-Based Action Recognition") builds one graph per short time window where nodes are joints, and adds edges both within a frame (anatomical joint connectivity, structurally analogous to a "structural" graph) and between corresponding joints in adjacent frames (temporal edges) — collapsing space and time into a single joint spatiotemporal graph rather than treating them as two separate operators. This is a genuinely different design decision from DCRNN/STGCN's "GNN block + RNN/TCN block" factorization, and it is directly transferable to ROI time series: one could build a spatiotemporal graph with structural edges within each TR and temporal self-edges across TRs.

**Brain-connectome-specific architectures** mostly import these ideas rather than inventing new ones, and — importantly for this project — nearly all of them were designed for a static functional-connectivity matrix (correlation over an entire resting scan) rather than a genuinely dynamic time series flowing over a separate structural graph:
- **BrainNetCNN** (Kawahara et al., *NeuroImage* 2017) is CNN-based, not graph-based in the spectral/message-passing sense — it defines custom "edge-to-edge," "edge-to-node," and "node-to-graph" convolutional filters that exploit the adjacency-matrix structure of a connectome, predating the GNN wave but solving a related locality problem.
- **BrainGNN** (Li et al., *Medical Image Analysis* 2021) uses ROI-aware graph convolution (Ra-GConv) plus a learned ROI-clustering/selection pooling layer (R-pool) for interpretability, applied to (typically static) functional connectivity for classification.
- **BrainGB** (Cui et al., arXiv:2204.07054, 2022, published in IEEE TMI) is not a new architecture but a benchmark and modular library standardizing GNN design choices (node feature construction, message passing, pooling) specifically for brain networks across both structural and functional modalities — useful as a design-space reference rather than a model to adopt wholesale.
- **Brain Network Transformer** (Kan, Dai, Cui, Zhang, Guo & Yang, NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2210.06681) tokenizes each ROI by its row of the connectivity matrix (its "connection profile") and applies transformer self-attention across ROIs — a graph-transformer-style approach to a static connectivity matrix, not a temporal model.
- **BolT** (Bedel et al., *Medical Image Analysis* 2023, arXiv:2205.11578, "Fused Window Transformers for fMRI Time Series Analysis") is, notably, a genuinely temporal model: it applies windowed transformer attention directly to raw BOLD time series (not a precomputed static FC matrix), with a fused-window mechanism to progressively integrate local-to-global temporal context — but it does not explicitly condition on a separate structural graph; the connectome is absent from the architecture.
- **GraphS4mer** (Tang, Dunnmon, Qu, Saab, Baykaner, Lee-Messer & Rubin, arXiv:2211.11176, presented at ICLR/MLHC-adjacent venues 2023) is the closest published architecture to the exact combination this project needs, though built for EEG/ECG rather than fMRI: it stacks S4 (structured state-space, Gu, Goel & Ré's long-sequence architecture) layers per-channel for long-range temporal dependencies, then a graph structure learning (GSL) module that *dynamically* estimates the adjacency matrix from learned temporal embeddings (rather than taking it as fixed input), then GNN layers over that learned graph. This is architecturally close to "S4 or Mamba-style state-space block for the temporal signal + GNN for the (learned, here fixed) spatial structure," which is a natural template to adapt: swap GraphS4mer's *learned* adjacency for the project's *fixed, tractography-derived* structural adjacency, and the architecture becomes a direct answer to the project's stated combination problem.

Two structure-function-coupling papers are worth flagging as adjacent but distinct: Sarwar, Ramamohanarao & Zalesky-style and Neudorf, Kress & Borowsky's **"Structure Can Predict Function in the Human Brain"** (*Brain Structure and Function*, 2021; preprint bioRxiv 2021.03.15.435531) use a GNN to predict static functional connectivity and centrality measures *from* structural connectivity on HCP data, reporting the GNN explaining up to ~89% of variance in group-mean functional connectivity (much less, ~56%, at the individual level) — this is architecturally simple (feedforward/GNN regression) but paradigmatically important: it is a direct precedent for treating "predict function from structure" as a supervised regression problem on this exact dataset population (HCP), and its individual-vs-group variance gap is a useful benchmark/caution for the project.

## 3. Learning Paradigms, Organized by Objective (Not Architecture)

This is the PML-style cut: for each paradigm, name the probabilistic quantity being estimated, then note which architectures happen to have been used to estimate it — because in every case the architecture is a swappable "block."

### (a) Supervised discriminative: modeling p(y | x)

Here x is some function of the structural graph and/or functional time series (a static FC matrix, a learned embedding, raw ROI time series) and y is a discrete or continuous downstream label — diagnosis, age, cognitive score, task condition. This is by far the dominant paradigm in the neuroimaging-GNN literature: BrainNetCNN, BrainGNN, BrainGB's benchmarked models, and Brain Network Transformer are all trained end-to-end to minimize a supervised classification or regression loss. The "block" doing the p(y|x) approximation is interchangeable — BrainGB explicitly demonstrates this by benchmarking many GNN variants (GCN-style, GAT-style, edge-conditioned) under an otherwise-fixed supervised training pipeline and showing that the choice of message-passing/pooling scheme, not the paradigm, is what differs across their results. For this project, any "predict which movie clip / behavioral score / subject identity" task using the combined structural graph + functional signal falls in this bucket regardless of whether the p(y|x) block is a GNN, a GNN+RNN hybrid, or a graph transformer.

### (b) Self-supervised pretext tasks: modeling p(part of x | other part of x) without labels

The probabilistic quantity here is not p(y|x) for an external label but a pretext conditional or contrastive density defined purely from the data's own structure, used either as a pretraining signal or as the terminal objective (representation learning). Three distinct sub-families exist in the graph literature:

- **Contrastive learning** estimates something close to a density ratio between "true" and "corrupted/negative" pairs. Veličković, Fedus, Hamilton, Liò, Bengio & Hjelm's **Deep Graph Infomax** (ICLR 2019, arXiv:1809.10341) maximizes mutual information between local node ("patch") representations and a global graph summary, contrasting against representations from a corrupted (e.g., feature-shuffled) version of the same graph. You, Chen, Sui, Chen, Wang & Shen's **GraphCL** (NeurIPS 2020) instead contrasts two stochastically augmented views of the same graph (via node dropping, edge perturbation, attribute masking, or subgraph sampling), following the SimCLR template but with graph-native augmentations. Applied to this project, a natural analogue is contrasting two temporally-augmented windows of the same subject's movie-fMRI signal (or the same window under structural-graph-consistent vs. -inconsistent message passing) as positive/negative pairs — several brain-specific instantiations of exactly this exist, e.g. a temporal contrastive self-supervised approach pretraining on a subset of HCP task-fMRI runs and transferring to held-out tasks with drastically reduced label requirements, and graph-contrastive frameworks using diffusion-based augmentation of functional connectivity for brain-disorder detection.
- **Generative/reconstructive self-supervision** estimates an explicit conditional p(masked part | visible part). Hou, Liu, Cen, Dong, Yang & Tang's **GraphMAE** (KDD 2022, arXiv:2205.10803) masks node features and trains a GNN encoder-decoder to reconstruct them with a scaled cosine reconstruction loss, showing generative (masked feature reconstruction) pretraining can match or beat contrastive pretraining on graphs — directly analogous to masked-token modeling (BERT) or masked-autoencoding (MAE) in vision/NLP, just with the graph structure providing the context for imputation.
- **Temporal/sequential self-supervision on the signal itself**, independent of the graph — next-step or masked-timepoint prediction on ROI time series, or contrastive predictive coding (CPC)-style objectives distinguishing true future windows from distractors — is comparatively rare as a *native* brain-connectome contribution; most of what exists imports these ideas directly (e.g., paired-sequence self-supervised pretraining for fMRI transfer to brain-decoding tasks, arXiv:2305.09057), and none of the brain-specific papers found combine a temporal CPC-style pretext task with an explicit fixed structural-connectome prior in the same model — this is a concrete gap the project's paired dataset could fill.

### (c) Generative/predictive paradigms: modeling p(x) or p(future | past, structure)

Two distinct probabilistic targets get called "generative" in this literature and should not be conflated:

- **Forecasting**, i.e., p(future ROI signal | past ROI signal, structural graph), is a predictive-density objective, typically trained by minimizing a forecasting loss (MSE/NLL on future timepoints) rather than by learning a full joint distribution. This is exactly what DCRNN and STGCN do for traffic sensors, and GraphS4mer's architecture (minus its GSL module, replaced by the fixed structural adjacency) is a natural fit for doing the analogous thing on ROI BOLD signal: forecast future movie-fMRI activity at each ROI conditioned on the past signal and the fixed structural graph. This paradigm has a clean neuroscientific interpretation too: forecasting error under a structure-informed model is itself a candidate measure of structure-function coupling, complementary to the static regression approach of the "Structure Can Predict Function" line of work.
- **Generative modeling of graphs themselves**, i.e., p(graph) or p(node features, adjacency) jointly, is a separate objective aimed at *synthesizing* new graphs rather than predicting a signal on a fixed one. Score-based/diffusion approaches — Niu, Song, Song, Grover, Zhao & Ermon's **EDP-GNN** (AISTATS 2020, arXiv:2003.00638) and Jo, Lee & Hwang's **GDSS** (ICML 2022, arXiv:2202.02514, "Score-based Generative Modeling of Graphs via the System of Stochastic Differential Equations") — learn to reverse a noising diffusion process over both node features and the adjacency matrix jointly via a system of SDEs, enabling sampling of entirely new graphs from the learned distribution. For this project this paradigm is more speculative: it would apply if the research question were "generate plausible structural connectomes" or "denoise/impute a partially observed connectome," not to predicting function from a given structure. It is included here mainly so the report doesn't understate how large the "generative" bucket has become in the general graph-ML literature, and to flag that it is a substantively different question from forecasting.

### (d) Reinforcement learning framings

The honest finding here is that RL is a minority paradigm for brain-graph modeling, and where it appears it is used for architecture/graph-construction search or for interpretability, not for modeling the structure-function relationship itself:
- Zhao et al.'s **BN-GNN** ("Deep Reinforcement Learning Guided Graph Neural Networks for Brain Network Analysis," *Neural Networks* 154, 2022, arXiv:2203.10093) uses an RL agent to search over the number of GNN feature-propagation layers (effectively an architecture/hyperparameter search) tailored per brain network, rather than using RL as the model of brain dynamics itself — the probabilistic quantity being optimized is really a policy over architecture choices, evaluated by downstream classification accuracy, with the classifier still a standard supervised p(y|x) block.
- Reinforcement learning has also been proposed for the graph-construction step upstream of any GNN — e.g., learning a policy for adaptive edge/threshold selection when building a connectivity graph from correlation matrices, instead of an arbitrary fixed threshold — but this is graph-preprocessing automation, not a model of the structure-function relationship.
- **Neurofeedback** is the one place RL appears as a genuine model of a brain process rather than an ML engineering tool: neurofeedback experiments (where a participant learns to self-regulate neural activity given feedback) have been explicitly reframed through the lens of RL theory — credit assignment, reward prediction error, exploration — in the review "Neurofeedback through the lens of reinforcement learning" (Sitaram, Ros, Stoeckel et al.-adjacent literature; ScienceDirect/PubMed 2022, PMID 35550813). This is conceptually interesting but not directly applicable to the project's data: it models the human participant's brain as the RL agent during real-time feedback, which presupposes an online/closed-loop experimental paradigm that naturalistic movie-watching fMRI does not have.
- No credible paper was found that uses RL to model the temporal evolution of functional signal over a fixed structural graph as a sequential decision process (e.g., treating each ROI's future state as an action or treating attention-over-structural-neighbors as a policy) — if the project wants an RL framing of structure-function coupling itself, that would be a genuinely novel contribution rather than an application of existing published work, and should be described to any audience as such rather than implied to have precedent.

## 4. Connecting Back to the PML Frame

Murphy's organizing move — a network is a parametric approximation to one piece of a probabilistic model, and the real design questions are (i) what is the target quantity and (ii) what does the loss actually estimate — maps onto this literature cleanly:

| Paradigm | Quantity being modeled | Example "blocks" used (interchangeable within the paradigm) |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised discriminative | p(y \| x), x = graph/signal-derived features | GCN, GAT, BrainNetCNN's custom filters, Brain Network Transformer's attention — any of these can sit under the same cross-entropy/MSE loss |
| Self-supervised contrastive | An implicit density ratio between true and corrupted/augmented pairs (InfoNCE-style bound on mutual information) | GNN encoder in DGI/GraphCL; equally could be an RNN/Transformer encoder over ROI time series |
| Self-supervised generative/masked | p(masked features \| visible features, graph) | GNN encoder-decoder in GraphMAE; could be replaced by a Transformer encoder-decoder with no change to the objective |
| Forecasting | p(future signal \| past signal, structural graph) | DCRNN's GNN+GRU, STGCN's GNN+temporal-conv, GraphS4mer's GNN+S4 — three different blocks solving the identical predictive-density objective |
| Graph generation | p(graph) jointly over nodes/edges | Score-based diffusion network in EDP-GNN/GDSS — architecture is a denoising network, paradigm is score-matching/diffusion |
| RL (architecture/graph search) | A policy π(architecture or graph-edit \| state), optimized by a downstream reward (e.g., classification accuracy) | Any RL agent (e.g., a small MLP/RNN policy) — the "block" here is almost incidental; the paradigm shift is that the training signal is a reward, not a differentiable loss |
| RL (neurofeedback, brain-as-agent) | The participant's own neural policy, inferred from behavior under a reward signal | N/A — this models the brain, not an artificial network |

The recurring, load-bearing observation across essentially every architecture paper surveyed above is that swapping the block (GCN → GAT → Transformer, or RNN → S4/state-space) inside a fixed paradigm produces incremental gains, while swapping the paradigm (supervised → contrastive → forecasting) changes what the model can be asked to do and what data efficiency looks like — GraphCL's ablations and the temporal-contrastive fMRI pretraining result (12 vs. 100 subjects to converge) are two concrete, cited instances of this. For a project with ~174 paired subjects, that is the practically important axis: an architecture upgrade is bounded in what it can buy, whereas moving from purely supervised to a self-supervised or predictive-density pretraining objective changes the effective sample-efficiency regime.

## 5. Most Relevant / Foundational Papers

1. **Bruna, J., Zaremba, W., Szlam, A., & LeCun, Y. (2014).** "Spectral Networks and Locally Connected Networks on Graphs." ICLR 2014, arXiv:1312.6203. — First generalization of convolution to graphs via the Laplacian eigenbasis; the historical origin point of the spectral GNN lineage.
2. **Defferrard, M., Bresson, X., & Vandergheynst, P. (2016).** "Convolutional Neural Networks on Graphs with Fast Localized Spectral Filtering." NeurIPS 2016, arXiv:1606.09375. — ChebNet; introduces Chebyshev-polynomial spectral filters with linear complexity and explicit K-hop localization, and explicitly names brain connectomes as a motivating irregular domain.
3. **Kipf, T. N., & Welling, M. (2017).** "Semi-Supervised Classification with Graph Convolutional Networks." ICLR 2017. — The first-order GCN simplification of ChebNet that became the default GNN layer for half a decade, including early brain-network GNNs.
4. **Veličković, P., Cucurull, G., Casanova, A., Romero, A., Liò, P., & Bengio, Y. (2018).** "Graph Attention Networks." ICLR 2018, arXiv:1710.10903. — Introduces learned attention over graph neighborhoods, moving the field from fixed spectral weights toward flexible, inductive message passing.
5. **Bronstein, M. M., Bruna, J., Cohen, T., & Veličković, P. (2021).** "Geometric Deep Learning: Grids, Groups, Graphs, Geodesics, and Gauges." arXiv:2104.13478. — The unifying "equivariance blueprint" that frames CNNs/GNNs/Transformers/RNNs as one family; the direct conceptual bridge to the PML "interchangeable blocks" framing used in this report.
6. **Li, Y., Yu, R., Shahabi, C., & Liu, Y. (2018).** "Diffusion Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network: Data-Driven Traffic Forecasting." ICLR 2018, arXiv:1707.01926. — Canonical fixed-graph-plus-temporal-signal architecture (graph diffusion convolution nested in a recurrent encoder-decoder); the direct structural template for combining a static structural connectome with dynamic ROI time series.
7. **Yan, S., Xiong, Y., & Lin, D. (2018).** "Spatial Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks for Skeleton-Based Action Recognition." AAAI 2018. — Alternative template that fuses spatial and temporal edges into one spatiotemporal graph rather than factorizing GNN and RNN/TCN as separate stages.
8. **Kawahara, J., Brown, C. J., Miller, S. P., et al. (2017).** "BrainNetCNN: Convolutional Neural Networks for Brain Networks; Towards Predicting Neurodevelopment." *NeuroImage*, 146, 1038–1049. — Earliest widely-cited deep architecture custom-built for connectome adjacency-matrix structure (edge-to-edge/edge-to-node/node-to-graph filters), predating the GNN wave in neuroimaging.
9. **Li, X., Zhou, Y., Dvornek, N., et al. (2021).** "BrainGNN: Interpretable Brain Graph Neural Network for fMRI Analysis." *Medical Image Analysis*, 74, 102233. — Representative supervised-discriminative GNN for brain networks, with built-in ROI-clustering pooling for interpretability.
10. **Tang, S., Dunnmon, J. A., Qu, L., Saab, K. K., Baykaner, T., Lee-Messer, C., & Rubin, D. L. (2022/2023).** "Modeling Multivariate Biosignals With Graph Neural Networks and Structured State Space Models" (GraphS4mer). arXiv:2211.11176. — Closest existing architecture to "state-space temporal block + GNN spatial block," directly adaptable to fixed structural connectome + ROI time series by replacing its learned graph-structure module with the project's fixed tractography-derived adjacency.
11. **Veličković, P., Fedus, W., Hamilton, W. L., Liò, P., Bengio, Y., & Hjelm, R. D. (2019).** "Deep Graph Infomax." ICLR 2019, arXiv:1809.10341. — Foundational graph contrastive/mutual-information self-supervised objective; the conceptual root of graph contrastive learning applied to brain networks.
12. **Neudorf, J., Kress, S., & Borowsky, R. (2021).** "Structure Can Predict Function in the Human Brain: A Graph Neural Network Deep Learning Model of Functional Connectivity and Centrality Based on Structural Connectivity." *Brain Structure and Function* (preprint bioRxiv 2021.03.15.435531). — Direct precedent, on HCP data, for supervised regression from structural to functional connectivity, with an explicit and informative group-vs-individual variance-explained gap.

*Note on citation confidence: every paper above was verified against at least one independent source (arXiv abstract page, publisher page, or a citing survey) during this review. The neurofeedback-as-RL claim (Section 3d) is based on a specific, verifiable 2022 review article rather than a single named seminal paper, since neurofeedback-RL is a framing applied across a body of experimental work rather than one architecture paper. No RL paper modeling structure-function coupling itself (as opposed to architecture search or neurofeedback) was found; this absence is reported as a finding, not papered over.*
